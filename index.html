04Selected work

Four problems, taken apart.

No confidential data appears here — methods and outcomes only. Each study follows the same anatomy: the problem, my role, the system built, why it mattered, and what it signals.

01 The attrition no one could explain Root-cause diagnosis → range recalibration

The problem. Roles across Cisco's APJC region — spanning go-to-market, operations, and engineering — were losing people at an abnormal rate, and the losses didn't map to any one team, manager, or function.

My role. I spotted the pattern and owned the diagnosis.

The system. Cross-referencing attrition against compensation positioning showed the real mechanism: market reference ranges for the region had quietly drifted below peer benchmarks. The problem wasn't culture or management — it was pricing. I drove a full recalibration of the affected ranges.

Business value. Retention problems get expensive twice: once in departures, once in the wrong fixes. Repricing the ranges addressed the cause rather than the symptoms.

Signal — I look for the system underneath the symptom.

02 Rebuilding job architecture, with AI Hundreds of roles → one structure, every BU signed

The problem. Ahead of a Workday implementation, Cisco's job architecture — the families, groups, and titles that everything from pay ranges to career paths hangs on — needed a full refresh.

My role. I led the refresh for my scope and carried it through alignment.

The system. I used AI tooling to scrape and structure hundreds of role cards, then organized them into new job families, family groups, and titles. The harder half was human: securing sign-off from compensation leads across every Cisco business unit, before the structure was committed to the system of record.

Business value. Job architecture is load-bearing infrastructure — get it wrong and every downstream comp decision inherits the error. It shipped aligned, on time, ahead of the Workday cutover.

Signal — AI for the volume, judgment for the taxonomy, patience for the politics.

03 The exception-hiring model Three data sources → one decision interface

The problem. Out-of-range hiring exceptions were decided with fragmented evidence — market data in one place, internal range positioning in another, headcount distribution in a third.

My role. I designed and shipped the tool myself.

The system. A modeling interface that put external market data, internal range positioning, and headcount by management level side by side — one screen, built hands-on with AI tooling rather than waiting in an engineering queue.

Business value. Exception decisions moved from assembled-by-hand to answered-on-sight, with the trade-offs visible instead of implied.

Signal — when the tool doesn't exist, I build it.

04 Mapping Splunk onto Cisco Acquisition → comp integration dashboard

The problem. Cisco's acquisition of Splunk brought thousands of employees whose roles, levels, and pay structures had to be reconciled with Cisco's own.

My role. I built the comparison layer for compensation decisions.

The system. A Tableau dashboard mapping acquired Splunk roles against Cisco's job structure, giving comp decision-makers a shared view of how the two organizations actually lined up.

Business value. M&A comp integration runs on exactly this mapping; done poorly, it seeds years of internal-equity problems. The dashboard made the decisions inspectable.

Signal — I turn messy organizational data into instruments people decide with.

05Method

How I work an ambiguous problem.

Reverse-engineered from the work above — each step is something I have actually done, not a framework borrowed from a slide.

  1. Notice the anomaly.The APJC attrition didn't map to any team. That mismatch was the signal.
  2. Trace it to the system.Not "people are leaving" — reference ranges had drifted below market.
  3. Name the decision.Executives don't need analysis; they need the choice framed. Recalibrate, or keep paying for attrition.
  4. Build the instrument.A model, a dashboard, an architecture — whatever makes the trade-offs visible on one screen.
  5. Win the room.Every-BU sign-off on job architecture wasn't a technical achievement. It was thirty conversations.
  6. Ship it into the system of record.Analysis that never reaches Workday, Tableau, or the planning cycle didn't happen.

07The AI argument

Why this background compounds now.

Every large company sits on the same buried asset: years of fragmented workforce, compensation, organizational, and financial data. AI has abruptly changed what that data can do — ranges that recalibrate against live markets, job architecture that stays current instead of decaying between redesigns, exception decisions that arrive with their evidence attached.

But the models don't know the org. Turning that possibility into working systems still requires someone who understands the data and the incentives around it — how comp committees actually decide, what Workday will and won't tolerate, why a technically correct taxonomy dies in review, and how a range drifts below market three years before anyone notices the attrition.

That translation layer is where I've already been working. The job-architecture refresh, the exception-hiring model, the acquisition mapping — each one was AI or analytics applied to live compensation infrastructure, carried through the human alignment that determines whether systems get adopted or ignored. I didn't spend the last five years watching this shift. I spent them practicing it.
